Five rules that do most of the work
B–C and E–F have nothing in between
Between most letter names there is a sharp, one fret apart. These two pairs are the exceptions — they sit on neighbouring frets. Learn those two and the rest of the neck falls into place.
One fret is a half step
Every fret raises the pitch by the smallest step there is; two frets make a whole step. Every scale shape is a recipe of those steps, which is why the shapes move.
Two strings up, two frets up is the same note
That is the octave shape, and it works anywhere on the neck. Find a root and you instantly know a second place to play it.
The low B is the E string, five frets up
Any note on the E string also lives on the B string five frets higher — E is fret 5, G is fret 8. Learn the E string and the B string comes free.
C♯ and D♭ are one fret
One fret, two names; which one you use depends on the key. Under your finger it is identical, so do not let the double names put you off.

Reading the staff — bass clef in one minute

Bass guitar is written in bass clef, one octave above where it sounds — otherwise every note would drown in ledger lines. The five lines, bottom to top, are G B D F A (Good Bassists Deserve Fine Amps); the four spaces spell A C E G.

Anchor the open strings first and read everything else off them: open E hangs on the first ledger line below the staff, open A is the bottom space, open D is the middle line, open G is the top space — and the low B hangs below two ledger lines. A ♯ in front of a note head means one fret higher than the natural, and a ♭ means one fret lower — every ♯ has a flat name too: C♯ = D♭ — same fret, same sound.

A tab shows you the notes. This one listens to yours.

A scrolling tab player already does the hard part of learning a song — it shows every note, mutes the bass track so you can take its place, slows the song to a tempo you can play, and loops the bar that keeps beating you. Songsterr Plus does all four, and nothing here tries to compete with it: use it as the record. What it cannot do is hear you — a tab never knows whether the note you actually played was the note it asked for. That is the only job of this page.

So what you get here is a root-and-section roadmap: the order of the sections and the root to sit on, which is most of a root-driven rock bassline. Deliberately not a transcription — the fills and the exact notes live in the tab, and that is where they belong.

Setlist — songs back to back

A live set is not ten songs, it is the seams between them: song ends, eight beats of count-in, new key, go. Pick 3–4 songs and run them with the click — each at its own tempo — and the verdict watches exactly those entries.

Your rig: the record, the tab player, and what the app hears

The best source is a tab player, not Spotify. Spotify gives you the record at full speed with the bass still playing — you cannot hear whether you are in the way, and you cannot slow it down. In Songsterr Plus you get the four things that actually matter: mute the bass track so the part is yours, drop the tempo to something you can play, loop the section that keeps beating you, and see the notes while you do it. Anything with the same controls works — a backing track in a media player, a DAW, a looper.

Then the split is clean: the tab player shows and plays, this page listens. Its roadmap tells you which root the section wants; your bass tells it what you played. Nothing here grades your rhythm against the record — the tap-sync clock is not accurate enough to be honest about that, and the drill screen's click is, so timing lives there.

With the bass DI'd into the audio interface, this page hears only the bass — the interface sends it nothing else. So play the record as loud as you like, through headphones or through speakers: it never reaches the app, and it cannot be mistaken for a note you played.

Press Direct Monitor on the Scarlett and listen on headphones — you hear yourself with no delay at all, and the record comes from your computer's own output alongside it.

On a phone's microphone it is the other way round: the app hears the room, so the record bleeds straight in and detection gets unreliable — it will score the record's bass as if it were you. Use headphones rather than speakers, treat the score as rough, or use the app's click, which needs no record at all.
